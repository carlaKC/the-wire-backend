# Pitch — Demo 4: Evidence Quality

## What you're showing

Two tips about the same person — the CFO of a public industrial
manufacturer. One sender is the CFO's own executive assistant. The
other is an anonymous former employee. Both are accusing the same
person of the same thing. The system grades them very differently on
the strength of the evidence they provide.

## What to say

> "These two tips are about the same person. Same company, same
> allegation — that the CFO is putting personal expenses on the
> corporate card. Both of them got submitted to your tip line. What
> would you do with them?
>
> [Open tip 1.] The first tip is from his executive assistant.
> Six transactions. Dates. Dollar amounts. Vendor names. Hotel
> folio numbers. The names of his wife and kids. The codes she was
> told to use to disguise the charges. She has the receipts.
>
> [Open tip 2.] The second tip is from someone who used to work
> there. They heard things from a friend. They don't have
> documents. They never had access to the expense system. They
> can't tell you when, how much, or how they know.
>
> Both tips might be true. But only one of them is something you
> can publish. The system tells you that on the documents page —
> here. The first one rates high on evidence references. The
> second one rates low. An editor sorting the inbox by reportability
> sees the executive assistant's tip first.
>
> We don't treat every tip equally. The system grades evidence so
> the strong ones don't get buried under the weak ones."

## Why this matters for investigators

A flat tip-line treats a documented fraud the same as a rumor. An
investigative editor's most valuable resource is reading time. Demo 4
shows the system putting the documented tips at the top of the queue,
where an editor can actually act on them, and putting the unactionable
ones in a different bucket without throwing them away.
